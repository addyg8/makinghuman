I lean over my lab bench, my shoulder burning with lactic acid as I meticulously swivel my pipetting arm back and forth hundreds—no, thousands—of times. My lumbar spine pinches. My eyes squint as I peer closer to ensure that a microscopic amount of invisible liquid lands into the appropriate well of yet more microscopic invisible liquid. I glance at the waste container, stacked with endless heaps of sharp plastic used for but a second, now biohazards that cannot be recycled, as I simultaneously consider how much goes to waste if I mess something up. I can't help but wonder: when did science get so small?

It was not always like this. Although humans have been using tools for over 3 million years, they did not begin to keep a written historical record until 3,300 BCE. And so, to the naked eye, the bulk of human history remains unrecorded. This does not mean they do not have a story, however, itself a term which originates from the Latin *historia*.

The knowledge of our early indigenous ancestors lived in memory, preserved through generations of stories that merged the seen with the felt, the sensed with the perceived. What we now minimize to prehistory was, in fact, a vast and colorful landscape which defined the human story, as told and felt through song, dance, and art.

Some of these memories, more than we might expect, persist today. Indigenous narratives record long-term observations of landscapes and animal behavior that modern archaeologists continue to confirm. The Klamath Tribe of Oregon, for example, whose culture stretches back over 13,000 years, has oral histories detailing Mt. Mazama's cataclysmic eruption, which formed Crater Lake approximately 7,700 years ago.

So, what did science look like, before the rise of mass civilization?

It began as ontological pursuit, a courageous quest to understand the enigmatic nature of existence, driven by wonder and deep appreciation for this strange and beautiful world.

Indigenous knowledge systems, built through intimate observation of and relationship with their land, thus sought not to understand individual components in isolation. They sought to understand the role which each part played within the whole. They merged observations with lived experience. They sought symbiosis with a nature understood as a living, intelligent web of relationships greater than any one individual. They sought holism. The first scientists of this world were ecologists.

The ecosystem, described through the indigenous lens, resembles what western philosopher Immanuel Kant defined as the *Kantian whole*, an entity in which the parts exist both for and by means of the whole, and the whole exists for and by means of its parts. The body cannot survive if the lungs don't supply oxygen to the blood and the heart doesn't pump oxygenated blood throughout the body. If the lungs don't supply oxygen, the heart fails. If the heart has no oxygenated blood to pump, the lungs fail.

Every puzzle piece carries a purpose beyond itself, devoid of meaning when considered in isolation. It is only when enough pieces come together and you zoom out, that a picture begins to emerge.

This stands in stark contradiction to what modern western science has become. Today, science is less a pursuit than a race to extract utility. To isolate and reduce, to hack away at nature's puzzle pieces and break them down even further. Answering questions nobody asked, because the answers are profitable.

But what is it to extract from such a system without giving back, if not self-destruction? Every individual is, after all, part of the picture too.

How did this happen, you ask?

Over time, our ancestors got better and better at agriculture. Those who grew grain, far less perishable than root vegetables, could store surplus. But humans had never experienced material surplus before. While nature had always been abundant, it was also unpredictable. It could quickly take, and thus was a force to surrender to and revere, to operate in exchange with.

Evolution had not calibrated our brain's reward systems for the stagnation emergent from surplus, this taking without giving back. The hedonic treadmill, the tendency to always return to the same baseline level of happiness, made sure that once we felt or tasted too much power, we always wanted more. Rewards became predictable, boring even, and it hijacked our understanding of value itself.

Humans became addicted to hoarding. Addicted to value, while forgetting what is valuable: that which you actually want to tell stories about. Fear was no longer about survival, it was now existential. People, disconnected from their true nature, sought control. Dominion. They sought to reduce everything to self versus other, good versus bad. It was the only way to make other small and less scary.

The most violent and extended expression of this is colonialism, which saw to the downfall of the majority of Indigenous people and their land. Colonials, inflated by reductionist superiority, reduced complex societies to mere 'savages' and stripped them of their identity. They imposed their violent, limiting beliefs everywhere they could, for it was the only way to keep themselves big, while making everything and everyone else small.

In this colonial, capitalist, expansion-driven mindset, humanity forgot about constraints. As hierarchies dominated, those at the top got stuck on a hedonic treadmill manifest as utter greed. The goal was no longer synergy with the land, but rather dominion over it. The power hungry, willing to do anything for more, forgot to revere that which is greater than any single one of us: Mother Nature.

No longer mother, nature was now machine, a means to an end, a view which was explicitly defined during the scientific revolution by thinkers such as Francis Bacon.

It's no wonder, then, that science is dreaded by so many today. That it feels so cold. So barren, uninviting, and absolute. This is a symptom of a far greater disease. It isn't that people aren't curious, but that their curiosity has been stifled. Modern science tells us that we, as individuals, are inferior. Our personal experience doesn't matter, because the data says so. Because it can't be commodified. And if it can, then it shall be exploited.

And so we stay, enclosed and protected, in our temperature regulated cubes. We see the wonders of the world splattered across social media as we lay horizontal on memory foam. We post on TikTok about how bad ChatGPT use is for the environment, while video streaming puts even greater demands on data centers than AI. We sit back, defenseless, and watch as corporations suck this world dry, extracting every finite resource they can find, dumping the waste in the backyards of those who have been exploited for longest.

I don't think, however, that all hope is lost. I posit, even, that we are amidst the very start of another scientific and intellectual revolution.

Indeed, I believe the end of reductionism is underway. There is little left to hack down, so we must build back up. Embrace the whole.

We are identifying more renewable energy resources. We are moving towards preventative and regenerative care, both for human health and that of our planet. We are studying how things like stress, the immune system, and the gut-brain axis shape one other. We are encroaching upon an epigenetic revolution, discovering how our perception of reality both shapes and is shaped by our DNA, itself a library of stories. We now have the computational tools to model the complexity of our internal and external worlds.

The change has already begun, so we must seize the momentum. Redefine what abundance means. Seek balance, understanding, and curiosity. Create better, more equitable systems. Question our definitions and frameworks. Learn from our predecessors.

We have the capacity to tell entirely new stories.

So, please be curious. Tell more stories. Share the fingerprint of your knowledge. Circulate abundance. Believe that we have the capacity to change, and then help, in even the smallest of ways, to make it happen. Evolution is not linear, but this is how we evolve.
